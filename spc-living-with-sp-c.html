Living with SP‑C deficiency affects the whole family — not just the child. This section covers the practical side: medical care, home oxygen, school, daily activities, and finding support.

Staying on Top of Medical Care

  • Keep all follow-up appointments — even when your child is feeling well. Lung function can change quietly before symptoms appear.
  • Build a relationship with a pediatric pulmonologist experienced in chILD conditions.
  • Have a written action plan for when your child gets sick or symptoms suddenly worsen — know exactly when to call the doctor and when to go to the emergency room.
  • Keep an updated medication list and share it with every provider who sees your child.
  • Make sure all vaccines are current — see Preventing Infections for details.

Home Oxygen — If Your Child Uses It

  • Learn how to use, clean, and troubleshoot all oxygen equipment before your child comes home.
  • Keep backup supplies and have a clear plan for power outages.
  • Register with your utility company as a household with life-sustaining medical equipment.
  • Use continuous flow mode on portable concentrators for infants and young children — do not use pulse-dose until your care team confirms your child can reliably trigger it.
  • Replace nasal cannula tubing regularly and keep extra supplies on hand.

School and Daily Activities

  • Talk to the school early. Work with the school nurse, teachers, and administration before the school year starts.
  • Consider a 504 Plan or IEP for formal accommodations — rest breaks, elevator access, reduced PE requirements, or extra time to move between classes.
  • Provide the school nurse with a written emergency action plan and a current medication list.
  • Encourage your child to stay as active as their condition allows — physical activity supports lung function and wellbeing; work with your care team to set safe limits.
  • Limit exposure to cigarette smoke, wood smoke, strong cleaning products, and other lung irritants at home and school.
  • If your child is approaching college age, start early conversations about disability services and health accommodations at the college level.

Siblings and Family Life

  • Help siblings understand their brother’s or sister’s condition in age-appropriate ways — children often cope better when they understand what is happening.
  • Watch for signs that siblings feel overlooked or anxious — hospital stays and medical demands can be hard on the whole family.
  • Be open with extended family and close friends about what your child needs — people generally want to help but do not know how.
  • If the mutation is inherited, other family members may need genetic testing — talk to your care team about who should be tested.

Taking Care of Yourself

Caring for a child with a serious chronic illness is exhausting. Your health and wellbeing matter — both for you and for your child.

  • Connect with other chILD families through the chILD Foundation — families who understand what you are going through are one of the most valuable resources available.
  • Consider counseling or a support group for yourself, your partner, and your child.
  • Ask your hospital social worker about caregiver support programs and respite care options.
  • Accept help when it is offered — meals, school pickups, or simply someone to talk to.
You Are Not Alone

chILD is rare, which means many families feel isolated after diagnosis. The chILD Foundation connects families, funds research, and advocates for children with rare lung disease. Reaching out to other families who truly understand can make an enormous difference.

Financial and Practical Help

  • Ask your hospital social worker about help with equipment costs, insurance appeals, and travel to specialized care centers.
  • Your child may qualify for Supplemental Security Income (SSI) or other state and federal benefit programs.
  • Many children’s hospitals have financial counselors who can help navigate insurance coverage and appeals.
  • Keep records of all medical expenses — some may be tax-deductible.
