There is no cure for SP‑C deficiency right now. Treatment focuses on helping the lungs work as well as possible, reducing inflammation, and keeping your child healthy and growing. The right plan depends on how severe the disease is.

Breathing Support

Supplemental Oxygen

Extra oxygen through a nasal cannula or mask to keep blood oxygen levels in a healthy range. Some children need this only during activity or sleep; others need it all the time.

↓ Oxygen Equipment Guide (PDF) →

CPAP

A machine that gently delivers pressurized air through a mask to keep the airways open. Often used during sleep.

Ventilator

A breathing machine for severe cases where the child cannot breathe well enough on their own.

ECMO

A heart-lung bypass machine used only in critical cases. It adds oxygen to the blood outside the body while giving the lungs time to stabilize.

Medications

Several medications can help reduce inflammation in the lung tissue. Your child’s doctor will discuss what may work best for your child’s specific situation.

  • Corticosteroids — reduce lung inflammation. How well they work varies from child to child. Long-term use requires careful monitoring for side effects such as effects on growth and bone strength.
  • Hydroxychloroquine — a medication originally used for other conditions, used here for its anti-inflammatory effects. Children taking this medication need regular eye check-ups.
  • Azithromycin — an antibiotic that also has anti-inflammatory effects when used at low doses long-term.

Nutrition and Supportive Care

  • Good nutrition is essential — children with lung disease burn more calories just to breathe. Some children need a feeding tube to get enough nutrition to grow.
  • Treat infections quickly — respiratory infections can trigger sudden serious worsening. Contact your doctor at the first signs of illness.
  • Airway clearance therapy — techniques and devices to help move mucus out of the airways. A respiratory therapist can teach your family how to do this at home.
  • Monitor for complications — such as pulmonary hypertension, and treat them if they develop.

Lung Transplant

For some children with severe, progressive disease that does not respond to treatment, a lung transplant may be considered. This is a major decision that requires careful evaluation by a specialized team. Lung transplant has been done successfully in both children and adults with SP‑C deficiency.

Families should talk openly with their medical team about whether and when transplant evaluation is the right step.

Protecting Against Infections

Respiratory infections can trigger a sudden serious worsening. Stay current on all vaccines — including the annual flu shot and RSV protection when recommended. Ask your doctor when your child should avoid crowds or contact with people who are sick.

Research Is Ongoing

Scientists are developing new treatments that target the protein misfolding process directly — including drugs that help cells clear broken proteins more efficiently. Ask your child’s doctor about current clinical trials that may be appropriate.

Key Points
  • There is no cure yet — treatment manages symptoms and slows progression
  • Most children need some level of breathing support — from occasional oxygen to full ventilation
  • Anti-inflammatory medications can help but response varies widely
  • Good nutrition is as important as medication
  • Lung transplant is an option for severe cases that do not respond to treatment
  • Preventing and treating infections quickly is essential
