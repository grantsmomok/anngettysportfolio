Statement of Teaching Philosophy

Ann Gettys

Over my career I have taught in nearly every format: in-person lectures, hands-on laboratories, action centers for active learning, and online courses in both formal and informal education. My audiences have included university students, Oklahoma City Public Schools students in outreach programs, tissue bank staff in workforce training, and families caring for children with rare lung disease. I have presented to small community gatherings and to scientific meetings with more than 300 people in the room. These settings could not be more different, but I start each one with the same questions: Who is this learner? What do they already know? How will they use this information once they leave the room? My goal is to do more than deliver content. I want to help people become lifelong learners who are curious, ask good questions, and keep looking for answers long after a course or instructor interaction ends.

Adapting to the learner and the course

No single format works for every learner or every subject. A cadaver laboratory calls for guided, hands-on discovery. A lecture on a complex physiological mechanism needs clear explanations that scaffold the learner from basic knowledge to complex systems. An online module for a worried parent needs to be clear, brief, and available whenever they are ready to learn. It should also adapt to the learner’s health literacy and emotional state, because a parent absorbing a new diagnosis does not learn the same way as one who has been managing a condition for years. I choose the format that fits the content and the people in front of me, and I adjust as I learn more about them. When students needed more time to apply concepts than a traditional lecture allowed, I worked with the Human Physiology teaching team at the University of Oklahoma to develop a flipped, case-based version of the course. Class time went to working through problems together instead of hearing material for the first time. I am now contributing to an NSF-funded project developing HyFlex instructional materials, so students can learn in person or remotely without losing the quality of the experience. I am also designing fully online Human Anatomy and Physiology courses for OU’s RN-to-BSN program.

Making science clear, relevant, and memorable

Communicating science well does not require making it complicated. A first-semester physiology student, a pre-nursing student, and a parent learning to manage home oxygen each need a different starting point. I build from what learners already know, use plain language before technical vocabulary, and add complexity as their understanding grows. I want my classroom to be a place where students can be honest about what they do not yet understand, because that honesty is where real learning starts.

From there, I look for ways to make the material stick. People understand and remember information when they can see where it fits in their own lives, so I connect course content to the world students already live in, from clinical cases to the TV shows and social media they watch every day. I also look for ways to make abstract ideas physical. In my physiology classes, I have been known to have students stand up and act out the voltage-gated channels of an action potential or the pumping chambers of the heart. There is something remarkable about watching a room full of students coordinate into a depolarization wave, the way a crowd sends a wave around a stadium. Moments like that make an abstract mechanism something students can see and feel. When students start noticing physiology outside the classroom, they are building a habit of inquiry that will last well beyond my course.

Designing with intention and always improving

My doctoral study in Learning Experience Design and Technology gave me the theory and language for what I had long been doing intuitively. I design with cognitive load in mind: reducing extraneous processing, managing the essential processing that complex content demands, and fostering the generative processing that leads to deeper learning. Mayer’s principles of multimedia learning, including coherence, signaling, segmenting, contiguity, and modality, guide how I build materials, whether the product is a lecture, a lab activity, an online module, or a patient brochure. Learning experience design has also shaped how I approach the work itself. I begin with a learner analysis, design around the learner’s context and needs, and treat every course as an iterative design that improves through prototyping, formative evaluation, and learner feedback. I love to think creatively, and each semester I look at what my students show me and ask what could work better. As tools like AI change how students find information, I design learning experiences that value discovery and reasoning, the things a chatbot cannot do for them.

Learning alongside my students

Some of the most meaningful parts of my career have come from advising and mentoring. Many of those relationships have lasted long after the classroom, and my students have shaped me as much as I have shaped them. I returned to doctoral study after a long and varied career because I am a lifelong learner myself. I want my students to see that curiosity does not end with a degree. It is a way of moving through the world.
