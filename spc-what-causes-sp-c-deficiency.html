This section explains how SP‑C deficiency is inherited, and why two children with the same mutation can have very different experiences.

How Is It Inherited?

SP‑C deficiency follows an autosomal dominant pattern. Everyone has two copies of the SFTPC gene — one from each parent. With autosomal dominant conditions, just one changed copy is enough to cause disease. The other normal copy cannot make up for it.

In plain terms: If one parent carries the SFTPC mutation, each child has a 50% (1 in 2) chance of inheriting it — the same odds as a coin flip, with every pregnancy.

What If Neither Parent Has the Mutation?

Many children with SP‑C deficiency are the first in their family to have the mutation. This is called a de novo mutation — a new change that happened for the first time in that child, not passed down from a parent.
De Novo Mutation

If neither parent carries the SFTPC mutation, the chance of it happening again in a future pregnancy is very low — but not zero. A genetic counselor can help your family understand your specific situation and whether other family members should be tested.

Why Does Severity Vary So Much?

Two children with the exact same SFTPC mutation can have very different experiences. One may have a life-threatening breathing crisis shortly after birth. Another with the same mutation may not develop noticeable symptoms until childhood or even adulthood.

The most common SFTPC mutation is called I73T. Researchers believe that other genes, the immune system, and environmental triggers — such as respiratory infections — may all influence how severely the disease affects any one person. This is still an active area of research.

Summary
  • A mutation in the SFTPC gene causes pro‑SP‑C to fold incorrectly
  • The misfolded protein builds up in Type II cells, injuring and killing them
  • Cell death triggers inflammation and scarring in the lung tissue
  • Just one changed gene copy is enough to cause disease (autosomal dominant)
  • Many cases are de novo — a new mutation in that child, not inherited
  • The same mutation can cause mild or severe disease in different people
