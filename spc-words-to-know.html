Use this page as a reference any time you come across an unfamiliar word. Terms are grouped by topic — lung basics first, then SP‑C deficiency terms.

Part 1 — How Lungs Work

Alveolar Macrophages (say: al-VEE-oh-lar MAK-roh-faj-ez)

Immune cells that patrol the inside of the alveoli. They engulf and break down dust, germs, dead cells, and old surfactant — acting as the cleaning crew of the lungs.

Alveoli (Air Sacs) (say: al-VEE-oh-lie; one alveolus is al-VEE-oh-lus)

Tiny balloon-like pockets at the ends of the smallest airways. This is where oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out into the air.

Bronchi (say: BRONG-kye; one bronchus is BRONG-kus)

The two main airways that branch off the trachea — one going into each lung. They keep dividing into smaller and smaller tubes.

Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)

A waste gas produced by the body. It moves from the blood into the alveoli and is breathed out with each breath.

Diaphragm (say: DYE-ah-fram)

The dome-shaped muscle below the lungs that powers breathing. When it contracts, the lungs expand and air rushes in.

Gas Exchange

The process where oxygen moves from the alveoli into the blood and carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli.

Lamellar Bodies (say: lah-MEL-ar BOD-eez)

Small storage compartments inside Type II cells where surfactant is packaged before being released into the alveolus.

Oxygen (O₂)

A gas in the air we breathe that the body needs to survive. It crosses from the alveoli into the blood during gas exchange.

Pulmonary Surfactant (say: PUL-moh-nair-ee sur-FAK-tant)

A mixture of fats and proteins that coats the inside of the alveoli. It reduces surface tension and keeps the alveoli open for breathing.

Surface Tension

The pulling force created when water molecules attract each other at an air-water boundary. In the lungs, it tries to collapse the alveoli.

Trachea (say: TRAY-kee-uh)

The windpipe — the main airway that carries air from the nose and mouth down into the lungs.

Type I Cells

Thin, flat cells that line most of the alveolar wall. They form the barrier that oxygen and carbon dioxide pass through during gas exchange.

Also called Type I pneumocytes (say: nyoo-MOH-sites). The word comes from the Greek pneumo meaning lung, and cyte meaning cell — so a pneumocyte is literally a “lung cell.”

Type II Cells

Specialized cells in the alveolar walls that produce, store, and release pulmonary surfactant. They are the key cells affected in SP‑C deficiency.

Also called Type II pneumocytes (say: nyoo-MOH-sites). The word comes from the Greek pneumo meaning lung, and cyte meaning cell — so a pneumocyte is literally a “lung cell.”

Part 2 — SP‑C Deficiency

Autosomal Dominant (say: aw-toh-SOH-mul DOM-ih-nant)

An inheritance pattern where just one changed copy of a gene is enough to cause disease. Each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting it.

chILD (Children's Interstitial and Diffuse Lung Disease)

A group of rare lung conditions in children where the lung tissue itself — not just the airways — is affected. SP‑C deficiency is one type of chILD.

Clubbing

Widening and rounding of the fingertips over time. A sign of long-standing low oxygen levels in the blood.

CPAP (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure) (say: SEE-pap)

A machine that gently delivers pressurized air through a mask to keep the airways and alveoli open. Often used during sleep.

Cyanosis (say: sy-ah-NOH-sis)

A blue or gray color of the lips, skin, or nail beds caused by low oxygen levels in the blood.

De Novo Mutation (say: day NOH-voh — Latin for “new”)

A gene change that occurs for the first time in a child — not inherited from either parent.

Exacerbation (say: ex-ZAS-er-BAY-shun)

A sudden worsening of symptoms, often triggered by a respiratory infection.

Fibrosis (say: fy-BROH-sis)

Scarring of lung tissue that makes the lungs stiff and reduces their ability to expand fully.

Gene

A section of DNA that contains instructions for making a specific protein. Everyone has two copies of each gene — one from each parent.

Interleukin (say: in-ter-LOO-kin)

A type of signaling protein released by immune cells. Interleukins act as messengers that tell other immune cells to start or increase an inflammatory response. In SP‑C deficiency, damaged Type II cells can release interleukins that trigger ongoing inflammation in the lungs.

Mutation

A change in a gene that affects how a protein is made or how it works.

Pro‑SP‑C

The larger, unfinished form of the SP‑C protein made inside the Type II cell. Normally the cell trims it into the final working protein. In SFTPC mutations this process fails.

Pulmonary Hypertension (say: PUL-moh-nair-ee HY-per-TEN-shun)

Abnormally high blood pressure in the blood vessels of the lungs. A possible complication of severe or long-standing lung disease.

Pulse Oximetry (say: ok-SIM-eh-tree)

A painless sensor clipped to a finger that measures the amount of oxygen in the blood. Sometimes called a pulse ox reading.

SFTPC Gene

The gene that provides instructions for making surfactant protein C (SP‑C). A mutation in one copy of this gene causes SP‑C deficiency.

SP‑C (Surfactant Protein C)

A small protein in pulmonary surfactant that helps it spread evenly across the alveolar surface and stay stable.

Tachypnea (say: tak-IP-nee-uh)

Rapid breathing. In infants, more than 60 breaths per minute is considered tachypnea and can be a sign the lungs are working harder than normal.

Part 3 — Treatment, Transplant & Prevention

504 Plan

A legal plan from school that sets up health accommodations — things the school will do to keep a student safe and included. Based on the Americans with Disabilities Act.

BiPAP (Bilevel Positive Airway Pressure) (say: BY-pap)

A type of breathing support similar to CPAP but delivering two pressure levels — higher when breathing in, lower when breathing out. Often more comfortable for some children.

Donor

A person who chose to donate their organs after death. Lung transplants use lungs from a donor who has passed away.

ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation) (say: EK-moh)

A machine that temporarily does the work of the lungs — or the lungs and heart together — when they cannot function well enough on their own. Blood is drawn out, oxygenated, and returned to the body.

Feeding Tube

A tube used to deliver nutrition directly into the stomach or small intestine when a child cannot eat enough by mouth. Common in children with chronic lung disease who need extra calories to grow.

GERD (Acid Reflux) (say: jerd)

A condition where stomach acid moves back up into the esophagus, causing discomfort. Common in children with chILD and can make eating uncomfortable or worsen lung symptoms.

HEPA Filter (say: HEE-pah)

A type of air filter that removes very tiny particles — including many germs and allergens — from indoor air. Recommended in some homes of children with lung disease.

IEP (Individualized Education Program)

A school plan that provides special education services and support for children whose health or disability affects their learning.

Intubation (say: in-too-BAY-shun)

Placement of a breathing tube through the mouth or nose and into the trachea (windpipe). Used for short-term ventilator support, typically in the ICU.

Mechanical Ventilation

The use of a machine (ventilator) to help a child breathe — either fully taking over breathing or assisting each breath the child tries to take.

Nasal Cannula (say: KAN-yoo-lah)

A small, soft tube that delivers supplemental oxygen through the nostrils. The most common way oxygen is given to children at home.

Nirsevimab (Beyfortus) (say: nir-SEV-ih-mab)

A medicine given to babies and young children to protect against RSV. Ask your doctor if your child qualifies.

Noninvasive Ventilation (NIV) (say: ven-tih-LAY-shun)

Breathing support delivered through a mask or interface, without placing a tube in the airway. Includes CPAP and BiPAP.

Palivizumab (Synagis) (say: pal-ih-VIZ-yoo-mab)

A monthly injection that helps protect high-risk babies from RSV. Ask your doctor if your child needs it.

Rejection

When the body's immune system recognizes a transplanted organ as foreign and tries to attack it. Anti-rejection medicines are taken every day after transplant to prevent this.

RSV (Respiratory Syncytial Virus) (say: sin-SIH-shul)

A common virus that causes cold-like symptoms in most children but can be very serious — and even life-threatening — in babies and children with lung disease.

Tracheostomy (say: tray-kee-OSS-toh-mee)

A small surgical opening made in the neck to place a breathing tube directly into the trachea. Used when long-term ventilator support is needed.

Weaning (from ventilator)

The gradual process of reducing ventilator support as a child's lungs improve and they are able to breathe more on their own.
