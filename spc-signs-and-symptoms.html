This section covers the warning signs of SP‑C deficiency — from newborns through older children — and what complications can develop over time.

Signs in Newborns and Young Infants

Some children with SP‑C deficiency have serious breathing problems right after birth. These signs can look similar to what is seen in premature babies who lack surfactant — even though the baby was born at full term.

  • Breathing very fast — more than 60 breaths per minute
  • Visibly working hard to breathe — the chest, neck, or belly muscles pull inward with each breath (called retractions)
  • Low oxygen levels in the blood
  • Blue or gray color around the lips, fingernails, or skin — called cyanosis
  • Poor feeding and not gaining weight as expected
  • Nostrils flaring with each breath
  • A grunting sound when breathing out

Signs in Older Children

Not all children with SP‑C deficiency have symptoms at birth. Some develop signs gradually over months or years.

  • A dry cough that does not go away
  • Getting short of breath during activity or exercise
  • Tiring much more easily than other children the same age
  • Getting respiratory infections often, or taking much longer to recover than usual
  • Crackling sounds when the doctor listens to the lungs with a stethoscope
  • Widening and rounding of the fingertips over time — called clubbing

Complications That Can Develop Over Time

  • Lung fibrosis — scarring that makes the lungs stiff and harder to expand
  • Pulmonary hypertension — high blood pressure in the arteries of the lung
  • Exacerbations — sudden episodes where symptoms get much worse, often triggered by a respiratory infection
Key Points
  • Symptoms can appear at birth or develop slowly over months or years
  • A full-term baby with breathing distress at birth should be evaluated for surfactant disorders
  • Symptoms in older children are often mistaken for asthma or recurrent pneumonia
  • Infections can trigger sudden serious worsening — act quickly
