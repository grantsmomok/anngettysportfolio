This section explains how SP‑C deficiency is diagnosed — what tests are done, what genetic testing involves, and what the results mean for your family.

How Is It Diagnosed?

Diagnosis usually takes several steps. A doctor first notices signs of interstitial lung disease — on an X-ray, CT scan, or during a physical exam. A genetic blood test then confirms whether the SFTPC gene has a mutation.

Because SP‑C deficiency is rare, it is sometimes mistaken for more common conditions like asthma or recurrent pneumonia. Getting to the right diagnosis often takes time and may require a specialist experienced in chILD conditions.

Tests Your Doctor May Order

Test What It Does
Chest X-Ray A quick image of the lungs. May show cloudiness or abnormal patterns — or may look nearly normal in early disease.
High-Resolution CT Scan (HRCT) A detailed lung scan that can show patterns specific to SP‑C deficiency, such as hazy areas (called ground-glass opacity) or structural changes in the lung tissue.
Pulse Oximetry A painless clip on a finger that measures how much oxygen is in the blood. Often lower than normal, especially during activity or sleep.
Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs) Breathing tests that measure how much air the lungs hold and how well air moves in and out. Usually done in children age 5 and older.
Blood Tests Check overall health and help rule out other causes of lung disease.
Bronchoscopy with BAL A flexible camera looks inside the airways and collects a small amount of fluid to check for infection or other clues about what is happening in the lungs.
Lung Biopsy A small sample of lung tissue examined under a microscope. Only needed if the diagnosis is still uncertain after genetic testing. Should be done by a team experienced with chILD.

Genetic Testing — The Most Important Step

A blood test looks at the SFTPC gene for mutations. This is the most reliable way to confirm SP‑C deficiency. Results typically take 2 to 4 weeks.

Testing both parents is also recommended — to find out whether the mutation was inherited from a parent or happened for the first time in your child.
Getting the Right Diagnosis

SP‑C deficiency can look similar to other lung diseases on imaging tests. The SFTPC genetic test is the key step that confirms it. Work with a center experienced in chILD — misdiagnosis can delay the right treatment.

What the Genetic Test Results Mean

Result What It Means
Positive A mutation was found in one copy of the SFTPC gene. This confirms the diagnosis of SP‑C deficiency.
Negative No SFTPC mutation was found. This does not always rule out SP‑C deficiency — some mutations may not be detected by standard panels. Your doctor will guide next steps.
Key Points
  • Diagnosis often requires both imaging tests and a genetic blood test
  • The SFTPC genetic test is the most reliable way to confirm SP‑C deficiency
  • Testing parents helps determine if the mutation was inherited or de novo
  • Work with a chILD specialist — this condition is often misdiagnosed
