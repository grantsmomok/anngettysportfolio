Every child with SP‑C deficiency is different. Doctors cannot always predict how the disease will progress for any one child. This section explains what we know — and what remains uncertain.

A Note for Families

Reading about SP‑C deficiency can be frightening. Please remember that statistics describe groups of patients — they do not predict what will happen to your child specifically. Your child’s care team knows your child and is the best source of guidance for your family’s situation.

What to Expect

SP‑C deficiency does not follow the same path in every child. Some children have serious breathing problems from birth. Others develop symptoms slowly over months or years. A few children with very mild mutations remain relatively stable for a long time.

Even two people with the exact same SFTPC mutation — including family members — can have very different experiences. Researchers are still working to understand why.

Common Patterns of Disease Course

  • Stable with mild disease — some children, particularly those with certain mutations, remain relatively stable for many years with careful management
  • Slowly progressive — lung function gradually declines over time; the pace varies widely from child to child
  • Episode-based worsening — some children have periods of stability interrupted by sudden flares, often triggered by a respiratory infection; lung function may not fully recover after each episode
  • Rapidly progressive — a smaller number of children, particularly some with onset in infancy, have severe disease that worsens quickly and may require transplant evaluation early

Factors That May Affect the Outlook

No single factor predicts outcome on its own, but doctors consider several things when thinking about a child’s prognosis:

  • The specific SFTPC mutation and how severely it disrupts protein folding
  • Age when symptoms first appeared — though earlier onset does not always mean a more serious course
  • How quickly lung function changes over time on breathing tests
  • Whether sudden worsening episodes occur and how well the lungs recover afterward
  • How well the child responds to anti-inflammatory treatment
  • Whether complications such as pulmonary hypertension develop
  • Access to a care team experienced in chILD

SP‑C Deficiency in Adults

SP‑C deficiency is not only a childhood disease. Some people with SFTPC mutations do not develop noticeable symptoms until adulthood, when they may present with a form of interstitial lung disease (ILD) or progressive lung fibrosis. This means that parents and siblings of an affected child may also be carrying the mutation — which is one reason genetic testing of the whole family is often recommended.

Research and Hope

SP‑C deficiency is an active area of medical research. Scientists are working on treatments that target the misfolding problem directly — including drugs that help cells process broken proteins more efficiently, and gene therapy approaches that may one day correct the mutation itself.

Families who participate in research registries and clinical trials help move this science forward — for their own children and for future generations.
